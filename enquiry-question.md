# Enquiry Question

The question that organises a history unit: a single, arguable question that pupils work towards answering over several weeks, and that the final assessed piece asks them to answer. In our [[ks3-curriculum-redesign]] every one of the 15 units is built on one.

## Definition

> [!abstract]
> An enquiry question is a genuine historical question with more than one defensible answer, framed around one second-order concept, which every lesson in the unit helps pupils answer and which the final task asks them to answer in their own words.

## How it differs from a topic heading

A topic heading names content: "The Black Death", "The Industrial Revolution", "The ropeworks". It tells you what you will learn about, not what you will do with it. An enquiry question names a problem: "How far did the Black Death change life for ordinary people?", "Why did the ropeworks close?".

The practical differences:

- **Selection.** With a heading, everything about the subject is in scope, so units bloat. With a question, content earns its place by helping answer it. Our Year 7 booklets shrank by about a third when we rewrote them this way.
- **Sequence.** A heading suggests chronological order inside the unit. A question suggests building an argument: first the knowledge, then the factors, then weighing them.
- **Assessment.** The final assessed piece is the enquiry question itself, so pupils know from lesson 1 what they are working towards.
- **Second-order focus.** Each question targets one of the [[second-order-concepts]]: "why" for causation, "how far did ... change" for change and continuity, "how significant" for [[historical-significance]], "what can ... tell us" for evidence.

## What makes a good one

From writing 15 of them, and from the session at the [[historical-teaching-forum-2025-enquiry-session|Historical Teaching Forum in 2025]]:

1. Arguable. If the answer is a fact, it is a quiz question.
2. Answerable with the knowledge in the unit. "Was the Norman Conquest a good thing?" fails; "How did the Normans keep control?" works.
3. Phrased so a twelve-year-old understands it on day one.
4. One second-order concept, not three. "Why did the ropeworks close and how did it change the town?" was two enquiries; we cut the second half.
5. Concrete. People, places, dates, not abstractions.
6. Sustains six weeks. If pupils can answer it in two lessons, it is too small.

## Pitfalls we hit

- Questions that hide a topic heading: "What was the Industrial Revolution?" Rewritten.
- Leading questions with an obvious answer: "Why was the Black Death so terrible?"
- Teachers abandoning the question halfway through a unit and teaching the topic. The booklet fix is a short "back to the question" box at the end of each lesson ([[enquiry-booklet-style-guide]]).
- Desmond's objection: an enquiry is a six-week island. The timeline lessons are our answer, imperfect ([[chronological-framework]], [[ks3-curriculum-review-2025-06-25]]).
